\begin{abstract}
The Causal Attitude Network \citep{dalege2016} models an attitude as an Ising-family network of
interacting evaluative components. \emph{The headline is that the model was moved from a hosted solver
to native graph rules, and that the audit of that move corrected two of this paper's own earlier
findings.} It was reimplemented on this substrate \emph{twice}: first as a
hosted mean-field self-consistency solver running beside the graph, then --- one day later --- as
graph rules over graph data, in the existing vocabulary and with no new primitive in the compiled
runtime. Five findings carry that, and two of them correct this paper's own earlier revision. The
\emph{alignment} claim, that the dynamics had no term surface, has failed: they have one, and the
remaining boundary is consumer-side and about scope. The two-cycle oscillation reported as a
property of the borrowed mathematics was a property of the update schedule, and vanishes on every one
of $126$ re-tested cells under damping. The \emph{performance} question is still not the complexity
one it appears to be, and the ladder above mean field has now been climbed: approximate propagation
in both variants, with a two-sided result and a $20$--$200\times$ cost, and exact inference where the
treewidth permits it. Of two registered predictions, one is confirmed with a large effect and a
control, and one is \emph{reversed} --- and survives its own solver correction in direction while
losing it in magnitude. All of this is reported as measured rather than reinterpreted.
\end{abstract}

\section{What was moved, what a move would mean, and on what standard}\label{sec:moved}
An attitude in the CAN model is a network of evaluative components with pairwise couplings and
external fields, and its behaviour is the behaviour of an Ising system: relaxation toward low-energy
configurations, frustration where couplings conflict, and a temperature-like parameter governing how
sharply it settles.

\emph{Moving a formalism onto this substrate means more than making it run there.} The substrate's
claim --- the substrate is the one set out in \emph{The Law of Graphic Equalisation --- Theory}
(referred to below as Graphic Equalisation) --- is that decisions are taken by graph rules over graph
data; a model that computes its result in a compiled routine and hands back an answer has been
\emph{hosted}, not moved. The distinction is the whole of the alignment question below, and it is not
rhetorical: the two cases differ in what can subsequently be inspected, mutated, minted and reasoned
over by the rest of the account. It is also the criterion that decides Remark~\ref{rem:native}, and
the criterion was met after this paper's first revision said it had not been.

\paragraph{The standard, stated once.} Every figure below is a \emph{measurement} reported from this
programme's own record rather than from a published source. What is offered is a pre-registered
implementation record which is not circulated with these papers, and it is a claim a reader may
decline to credit on exactly the terms Graphic Equalisation sets for the same case. Each rung of that
record fixes its own commit, seed and sample before it runs, and reports what was compared, what was
found, and the scope the run holds under; it names no implementation identifiers, and neither does
this paper. The standard is not re-disclaimed at each remark. What each remark does carry is the
\emph{date} of the rung it reports, because the defect this revision repairs was a dating one.

\section{Alignment: reimplemented twice}\label{sec:alignment}
The model arrived as a hosted solver: a compiled mean-field self-consistency routine, exposed to the
host and driven by seed-resident network data. The rules written against it did nothing, and the
reason was worth having.

\begin{remark}[The hosted stage, and the null that dated within a day]\label{rem:hosted}
\emph{(Record of 13 August 2026.)} There was no term surface for the dynamics at all, so the fifteen
rules written against the network were inert --- and the
obvious diagnosis, missing or malformed match clauses, was not the reason. The relaxation existed only across a host-language boundary, so a
rule could not express it, could not be matched against it, and could not mint against it. The rules
were not broken; there was nothing for them to be written in terms of. Exactly one of the fifteen
described the \emph{recognition} half rather than the update half, and that one was wired with real
match, where and effects clauses using only the existing term vocabulary; it works on the production
path. \emph{So the boundary was not a wall}, and the narrow statement that followed --- what had moved
was the network's data and its results, not its dynamics --- was true for about one day.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The dynamics are native, and two engine findings came with them]\label{rem:native}
\emph{(Record of 13 August 2026.)} The update was then expressed in the existing term vocabulary as
five rules and four admissibility productions, with \emph{no new primitive added to the compiled
runtime}: eight tests, three of them live demonstrations with measured numbers. A concept's local field is the sum of its two field attributes and the coupling-weighted
states of its neighbours; a single rule commits the thresholded value of that field --- three-valued,
with a neutral band --- whenever it differs from the state held. The update is asynchronous by the
engine's own execution model rather than by arrangement: one binding is applied and committed per
pass, and the next match reads the graph as it now stands. Across twenty draws per condition it failed
to converge on \emph{none}, in either vocabulary and under either state alphabet, where the continuous
synchronous relaxation oscillated on $12/20$ coarse and $4/20$ fine draws. For the two-valued case the
absence of a cycle is structural rather than observed --- every committed flip strictly decreases a
bounded energy --- and for the three-valued case it is an empirical fact on these draws and is
reported as one. This is the criterion Section~\ref{sec:moved} gives for \emph{moved} rather than
\emph{hosted}, and it is met.

Two engine-level findings were made and corrected while building, and both belong to the machinery
rather than to the model. \emph{First}: where sibling rules compete to update the same kind of node,
the pass scheduler lets the first with a replenished candidate pool take every pass and starve the
others indefinitely. It presented as non-convergence and was diagnosed as its opposite --- nodes
frozen at their initial value, the system already at a reachable fixed point it could not reach ---
and was fixed by collapsing the sign branches into one rule that computes its target. \emph{Second}:
the decoder's search prefers a redundant composite explanation to a clean single exact match at equal
coverage, because a coverage tie is broken on accumulated fit rather than on fewest parts. That is an
Occam violation in a tie-break; it was worked around by scoping the codebook to one candidate at a
time, and is reported rather than fixed.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{What still runs compiled, and the one reason.} Of the six consumers remaining after that
build, two were migrated and four were not --- and the four share \emph{one} confirmed cause, not the
three separate ones an earlier audit had named. The rule engine has no per-network scope: firing it on
behalf of one network advances every other network in the graph, confirmed directly rather than
argued, by watching an untouched network's state move from $0$ to $1$ across a call made for a
different one. Every consumer built as a loop over networks in the host therefore cannot fire a native
rule without corrupting the networks its loop has not yet reached, and deciding when the engine should
fire relative to such a loop is a question this arc never set out to answer and did not. Sixteen
compiled functions were audited for removal and none was removed, because each still has at least one
real consumer --- the migrations are gated rather than unconditional, so both paths stay live. Across
the arc's full seven consumers: two fully native, one partly, four still compiled.

\section{The solver: what the oscillation turned out to be}\label{sec:solver}

\begin{remark}[The oscillation was the update schedule, and the record found it]\label{rem:found}
\emph{(Record of 13 August 2026.)} What was observed is not in doubt and reproduces exactly. On a
fixed eight-node draw with a fixed nonzero field per node and a fixed start, the relaxation returns
different states at $400$ and $401$ iterations (opposite-parity gap $5.615746$) and the same state at
$400$ and $402$: a persistent period-2 cycle against a same-parity control. This paper's first
revision reported that as a genuine binary between cohering and failing to cohere --- an operation
that either resolves or returns a determinate nothing --- already present, unexploited, in the
borrowed model's own mathematics, and so as an instance of the claim that such failure comes in one
manner and not in kinds (Graphic Equalisation, Proposition~\ref{GE-prop:decoherence-one}).

\emph{It was the update schedule.} The relaxation iterated
$\sigma_i \leftarrow \tanh\!\big(\beta(\sum_j J_{ij}\sigma_j + h_i)\big)$ fully synchronously --- every
node's update in a sweep reading the previous sweep's state --- which is the textbook source of
period-2 limit cycles in this family; a second, byte-identical copy of the same iteration was found in
a sibling routine that the theoretical audit preceding the fix had not named. Blending each freshly
computed value with its own prior value, at a factor registered in advance rather than tuned against
these networks, makes the same draw converge cleanly (gap $0.000000$); setting that factor to $1$
reproduces the old numbers bit for bit on the same binary. The before and the after are one draw and
one code change apart, not two experiments. Blending cannot move or remove a fixed point, only the
trajectory to one, which is why nothing that already converged changed --- and why the damped fixed
point is a nearby genuine one rather than the average of the old cycle's two phases, exactly as a
frustrated network with several fixed points should give.

The elimination is total across everything re-tested: $126$ cells --- the original 24-trial registered
sweep, previously $6/12$ and $8/12$ oscillating by condition; a 30-cell sharpening previously up to
$100\%$ oscillating at $N=32$; and a 72-cell extension past the registered scope to $N=64$ and
$\beta=4.0$ --- converge cleanly, every one, gap exactly zero. So the correction is precise, and it is
the useful part of the whole arc: \emph{the binary this account has elsewhere was not present in the
borrowed mathematics; what was present was a Jacobi iteration.} What is \emph{not} established is the
general claim. No scheduling-independent failure to converge was found anywhere this search looked,
and that is a bounded negative --- complete graphs only, $\beta \le 4.0$, $N \le 64$, one initial
condition per trial --- rather than a proof that none exists. One later rung of the arc still calls
the binary real and confirmed again; it ran on the uncorrected solver, and re-running its sweep under
the corrected one is named in the record as a follow-on it did not perform.
\end{remark}

\section{Performance: the complexity question is the wrong one}\label{sec:performance}
The natural objection to putting an Ising model anywhere is that its ground-state problem is hard.
\citet{barahona1982} makes the sharp version available: the planar two-dimensional problem
\emph{without} an external field is solvable in polynomial time, while the same planar problem
\emph{with} a field, and the three-dimensional problem either way, is NP-hard. Every network here
carries a nonzero field, so the hard case is the relevant one.

\emph{And it does not bind, because no exact ground state was being sought.} The hosted solver computes
a mean-field self-consistency fixed point --- polynomial per iteration, unconditionally, at every
network size. The actionable question was therefore never whether this scales exactly, but how good
the approximation is and when it fails to converge. That question now has an answer, and the answer
sharpens rather than removes the point: exact inference \emph{is} now sought, where the treewidth
permits it, and refused where it does not.

\begin{remark}[The ladder, climbed]\label{rem:hierarchy}
\emph{(Record of 14 August 2026.)} Mean field is the first and weakest step of a known ladder --- mean
field, then Bethe and loopy belief propagation, then Kikuchi and generalized belief propagation
\citep{yedidia2005} --- and it is weakest \emph{precisely} in the regime of loops, strong local
correlation and frustration, which is the regime the frustration and basin audits exist to detect.
This paper's first revision observed that an entire coherent piece of statistical-mechanics vocabulary
had been borrowed while the better half of the same literature sat one step away unused, with a caveat
attached: Bethe and loopy propagation are themselves known to lose stable solutions in the same
frustrated, field-bearing, low-temperature regime. The step was then taken, and the caveat was tested
rather than repeated.

\emph{Loopy propagation, both variants.} Max-product is the theoretically appropriate one here,
because everything else in this toolbox is organised around a joint configuration rather than a
per-node marginal; on the dense frustrated case it is never worse than sum-product across eight draws,
and on every draw where it converges it ties or beats a twenty-restart search. Its convergence
reliability then \emph{inverts across topology}: it converges on $5$ of $8$ dense adversarial draws but
only $4$ of $15$ real sparse ones --- the opposite of the naive expectation that sparser is easier ---
and the cause was measured rather than left as an anomaly, a sustained period-4 message oscillation
traced to one structural 3-cycle plus small-integer weights producing exact ties between candidate
scores. Sum-product converges everywhere ($15$ of $15$) and to worse joint states (mean frustration
$2.142$ against $1.693$ for the restart search): the textbook warning that a marginal argmax is not a
good joint configuration, measured here rather than cited. Neither variant reliably beats the restart
search on the deployed networks, and both cost $20$--$200\times$ its wall-clock per solve. So the
caveat held where it was aimed, and was not the whole story: the worse convergence is on the sparse
networks, where it predicted none.

\emph{Exact inference, where the width permits.} Off that ladder rather than on it, exact minimum-energy
inference by clique-tree message passing was built for low-treewidth networks. It agrees with literal
brute-force enumeration on five real networks, float-tight, and cross-checks against the same energy
function every other consumer uses; it refuses cleanly on the case whose treewidth is $15$, in under
two milliseconds and without mutating anything, rather than attempting an exponential search; and on a
real cycle-topology network it finds a strictly lower-energy state ($-5.743$ against $-5.102$) than
twenty random restarts. That last is the concrete content of what ``exact'' buys: a widened search
finds the best of the basins its restarts land in, and this does not search basins at all.

\emph{Tractability, measured.} The restart search exists because local-minimum risk was measured
first, and not only on the adversarial case: $14$ of $30$ real deployed networks ($46.7\%$) show
nonzero spread across twenty starts, so a sparse near-tree topology does not by itself guarantee a
benign landscape. Treewidth was measured exactly on both graphs --- $2$ for the real template, $15$
for the dense stress case --- which is what decides where exact inference applies at all. And the real
coupling matrix has effective rank $\approx 1.98$ of $5$: the couplings this model carries are
substantially less independent than their count suggests.
\end{remark}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\begin{remark}[The registered scaling prediction is reversed, and its magnitudes moved]\label{rem:null}
\emph{(Record of 13 August 2026.)} The primary registered prediction --- that coarse quantization would
yield fewer distinct basins than fine, with the gap widening in network size --- \emph{is not
confirmed, and the measured direction is closer to reversed}. As first measured, coarse yields
\emph{more} distinct basins ($6$ against $4$ at $N=4$; $15$ against $4$ at $N=8$), a higher
entropy-weighted effective basin count, and higher mean frustration ($2.10$ against $1.55$ at $N=4$;
$15.32$ against $11.06$ at $N=8$); at $N=16$ the two conditions converge to near-parity on every
measure. The manipulation check holds throughout --- coarse quantization does produce larger per-edge
representational error at every $N$ --- so the intervention worked and the prediction about its
consequence was wrong. That is the useful shape of a null and it is reported here rather than
reinterpreted into a different prediction that would have survived.

Those figures were measured on the uncorrected solver of Remark~\ref{rem:found}. The reversal was
subsequently replicated on five independent coupling draws per cell and the replicated cells re-run
under the corrected one. \emph{The direction holds}: the effective basin count is higher for coarse at
both sizes ($2.358$ against $2.145$ at $N=4$; $3.616$ against $2.088$ at $N=8$). \emph{The magnitudes
do not}: frustration falls by roughly $7$--$10\times$ at $N=4$ and $2$--$2.5\times$ at $N=8$, and the
effective basin count roughly halves, because restarts landing on different phases of an oscillation
had been counted as distinct basins. And one sub-claim does not survive at all --- the concentration
reading, that a coarse landscape is the less concentrated of the two, is gone at $N=4$ ($0.565$ against
$0.560$) and reversed at $N=8$ ($0.405$ against $0.575$), which retires the gloss that coarse belief is
the more easily knocked between alternatives. The single-draw frustration figures of the first
paragraph were not themselves re-measured; the re-check ran on the replicated cells.

Two further results the arc produced, both on the uncorrected solver and both carrying that caveat.
\emph{A null}: there is no crossover network size anywhere in $\{4,8,12,16,24,32\}$ under a criterion
registered in advance, on either the plain or the structured coupling design. The criterion never
fires, and the pattern does not approach it --- the sign of the effective-basin gap flips three times
across the six sizes. \emph{And the strongest positive result in the whole arc}, which is not about
bin count at all: the sharpest resolution effect measured anywhere is the presence or absence of a
\emph{neutral option}. A two-value vocabulary produces $1.6$--$2\times$ the frustration of every
vocabulary with three or more values, at every size tested ($22.6$ against a $13.9$--$15.3$ band at
$N=8$; $122.9$ against $48.4$--$61.9$ at $N=16$; $501.3$ against $246.9$--$275.2$ at $N=32$), while
three through eleven values show no reliable ordering among themselves. Under the corrected solver
this is the one earlier finding that does not merely survive but \emph{sharpens}, to $2.36\times$ at
$N=8$ and $2.71\times$ at $N=16$. Removing the neutral option is not coarsening a scale; it is removing
the representation's own way of declining to commit, and that is what the frustration is measuring.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Resolution separates the basins, with a control that says so]\label{rem:bigville}
\emph{(Record of 13 August 2026.)} A second registered run, on a population of simulated residents each
carrying its own network at its own representational resolution, confirmed both of its predictions.

The manipulation check is large: the coarse resident's mean rounding error is about $9.2$ times the
fine resident's ($0.553$ against $0.060$). The primary prediction --- deliberately not the naive
basin-count intuition, that having already been falsified above on the same engine --- is that under
\emph{identical} scenario evidence the coarse network relaxes to a positive-energy, highly frustrated
basin (frustration $9.86$, energy $+5.19$) while the fine one relaxes to a negative-energy,
low-frustration basin (frustration $1.62$, energy $-1.77$). It does.

A population-wide check across one hundred residents, reported as exploratory rather than
pre-registered to the same standard, shows the same direction on average (mean frustration $3.29$ in
the low-resolution group against $1.38$ in the high). \emph{The control is what makes this worth
reporting}: holding resolution fixed across the population collapses the trait-to-frustration
correlation from $-0.33$ to $-0.08$, so resolution rather than some correlated trait is carrying it.
\end{remark}

\section{Falsifiability commitments}\label{sec:falsifiability}
Each commitment below is marked \textsc{[shape]} or \textsc{[detail]}. A \textsc{[detail]} failing is a
revision: the account survives with that mechanism replaced. A \textsc{[shape]} failing costs
\emph{range} rather than a mechanism, because what fails is something the substrate is claimed to
require or to permit --- so the account does not hold where it claimed to, which is the more serious of
the two and is still a boundary rather than an annihilation.

Two of the five commitments made in this paper's first revision have \emph{fired} against the record
this paper reports from. Both are recorded as fired and replaced below, not quietly removed.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textsc{[shape]}~\textbf{The dynamics are native, and the remaining boundary is scope.} Fails if
the native rules' results diverge from the hosted solver's on the same network, or if the per-network
scoping cannot be enforced in rule matching. \emph{This replaces a commitment that fired.} The first
revision committed that the migration was partial in the way it stated --- ``fails if the dynamics can
be expressed in the existing term vocabulary without new term surface, that is, if the fifteen rules
were inert for an ordinary reason after all.'' They can, and they were, one day after the stage that
revision reported (Remark~\ref{rem:native}). The inertness diagnosis of Remark~\ref{rem:hosted} stands;
the conclusion drawn from it does not.
\item \textsc{[detail]}~\textbf{Resolution drives basin quality.} Fails if the frustration and energy
separation survives holding resolution fixed, or if the population correlation does not collapse under
that control.
\item \textsc{[detail]}~\textbf{The scaling prediction is reversed and not merely unconfirmed.} Fails
if the basin counts recover the registered direction at $N=4$ and $N=8$ under a larger sample
\emph{on the corrected solver}. The first revision's form of this commitment --- a larger sample on the
uncorrected solver --- has been run and did not fire: five independent draws per cell hold the
direction. It is restated here against the corrected solver, on which the direction also holds
(Remark~\ref{rem:null}) and the magnitudes do not.
\item \textsc{[shape]}~\textbf{Exact inference does not change what the separation says.} Fails if the
frustration and energy separation of Remark~\ref{rem:bigville} does not survive exact clique-tree
inference on the treewidth-2 networks where it applies. The first revision's form of this ---
``fails if an exact solver at these network sizes changes the reported quantities materially'' --- had
no instrument to fire against. It now has one (Remark~\ref{rem:hierarchy}), and running it against
Remark~\ref{rem:bigville} is the one thing on this list not yet done.
\item \textsc{[detail]}~\textbf{The oscillation is the solver's and not the model's.} Fails if a damped,
asynchronous, or independently written solver reproduces a period-2 cycle on any of the $126$ re-tested
cells. \emph{This replaces a commitment that fired.} The first revision committed, as
\textsc{[shape]}, that ``the oscillation is the model's and not the implementation's --- fails if the
two-cycle disappears under an equivalent solver written independently.'' It disappears under damping,
on every cell re-tested (Remark~\ref{rem:found}). The replacement is \textsc{[detail]} because what is
now claimed is a fact about one iteration scheme, not a shape the account requires.
\item \textsc{[detail]}~\textbf{Max-product's convergence deficit on the sparse networks is the
mechanism named.} Fails if breaking the tied candidate scores --- perturbing the small-integer weights
--- does not restore convergence on the networks where the period-4 message oscillation was measured.
\end{enumerate}
