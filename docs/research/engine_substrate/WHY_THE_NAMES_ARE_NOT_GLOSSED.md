# Why the fiction borrowings are not glossed

**2026-08-28.** The corpus names its central objects after fiction — the **Seldon manifold**
(Asimov), **Narrativium** (Pratchett), **Muchness** (Carroll), and the short form's epigraph
(Adams) — and glosses none of them. This note records why, because the reason is a position rather
than a preference, and because a reviewer will ask.

## The debt is real and is usually hidden

Fiction has seeded working science repeatedly, and the record is better than the field's habits
suggest:

- **Robotics** — Asimov coined the word in *Liar!* (1941), believing he was using an existing term.
  The discipline's name came out of a magazine story.
- **Geostationary relays** — Clarke, *Wireless World*, 1945. Credited, unusually: the orbit carries
  his name.
- **Waldo** — Heinlein's 1942 story gave remote manipulators the name the field still uses.
- **Rocketry** — Goddard, Tsiolkovsky and Oberth each named Verne or Wells as what set them going.
  Three independent founders of a field, one fictional source between them.
- **Cyberspace** — Gibson, 1982/1984. The word preceded the thing it now names.
- **The handheld phone** — Cooper has said in public, many times, that the Star Trek communicator was
  the target.
- **TASER** — an acronym for *Thomas A. Swift's Electric Rifle*, from the Tom Swift juveniles.
- **Grok** — Heinlein, 1961; now ordinary vocabulary in computing.

The pattern in the failures is consistent: where the borrowing is a **coinage** it survives, because
the word itself is the evidence and cannot be dropped. Where the borrowing is an **influence** it
tends to vanish once the idea becomes respectable, because citing fiction reads as unserious. The
loss is field-dependent — aerospace and computing are relatively open about it, physics much less
so — so this is a habit rather than a rule, but it is a widespread habit and it falsifies the record
in one direction only.

## Why glossing would perform the embarrassment

The obvious accommodation is a footnote: *"Seldon manifold, after Asimov's Foundation."* That is
exactly the wrong move, and seeing why is the point.

A gloss signals that the borrowing **needs justifying**. It is an apology in the grammar of a
citation, and it concedes the premise that using a name from fiction is a lapse to be excused. Not
glossing signals the opposite: that the borrowing is ordinary, that a reader is expected to
recognise it, and that no defence is owed because no charge stands.

So the policy is not *"people who know will know"* — that is only its effect. The policy is that
**explaining the name would enact the very embarrassment the practice is refusing.** Lampshading is
the load-bearing move: naming the thing openly and then declining to defend it is a stronger position
than either hiding it or apologising for it.

## The reach argument, which is the real one

Millions of people read Pratchett. They did not read Marx.

That is not a joke about seriousness; it is a claim about which codebook is actually shared, and it
is the strongest reason in this note. A name does compression work only if the reader already holds
the entry it points at. Borrow from a source the reader has read and the name is a **decode against a
held codebook** — recognition, followed by the technical refinement that narrows it. Borrow from a
source the reader has not read and the name is a **mint**: the reader must build the concept from
nothing, which most will not do, so the name costs more than a plain description would.

*Narrativium* works because a large readership already holds the shape — a world where narrative
exerts force, where being the seventh son of a seventh son is causally operative, where the story is
a load-bearing part of the physics. The paper then says: yes, that, made precise, as a measurable
capacity. One sentence of refinement against a held entry. A reader without the entry pays full
construction cost, and gets a slightly odd word.

The Marx half needs its own care, because the obvious objection is right. **Marx's influence is
plainly larger than Pratchett's**; what is smaller is his *first-hand readership*, which is the
thing that matters here. *Capital* is the canonical instance of a book more purchased than finished,
and its ideas reached most people who hold them through intermediaries. That distinction is exactly
the one that decides the naming question:

- **Influence transmits conclusions.** It gives a reader the answer without the shape, which is
  useless for naming, because a name has to point at a structure the reader can already feel.
- **Readership transmits shapes.** Someone who has read forty Discworld novels has the narrative-
  causality shape whether or not they could state it, which is precisely the condition under which a
  borrowed name is cheap.

So the choice is not between rigour and fun. It is between an **expensive shared prior** and a
**cheap** one, and academic naming routinely assumes a codebook most of its readers do not hold while
believing itself to be the careful option.

### The honest limit

The shared codebook is demographically shared, not universally. Pratchett's readership is large and
also skewed — anglophone, disposed to fantasy, weighted to particular decades. So the name is cheap
in frames that hold the entry and simply **absent** in frames that do not, which is this corpus's own
aperture distinction applied to its own exposition: below a reader's aperture the name is not a poor
signal, it is no signal. That is a real cost and it is not evenly distributed. It is accepted on the
grounds that the alternative — a name assuming familiarity with a theoretical literature — is cheap
for a strictly smaller set of readers.

## The second reason, which is not a lesser one

It is more fun, and that has consequences worth stating plainly. A corpus of nine papers about
substrate minimality is a hard read. *Narrativium* is a better name than *deformation-capacity
measure*: it is shorter, it is memorable, and a reader can hold it across three papers without
re-reading its definition. Naming that helps a reader carry an object is doing work, and the fact
that it is enjoyable does not subtract from the work.

## The known cost, accepted

Some readers will discount the whole corpus on the names alone. That has already happened once — the
recorded case is a reader responding to *Seldon manifold* with "extraordinary claims require
extraordinary evidence" without engaging the construction. The judgement here is that such a reader
was going to find a reason regardless, and that the names supply an early and cheap one rather than
creating the disposition. An easy out taken early is preferable to a late one, and it costs a reader
who was never going to arrive.

## Consequence for the papers

**Do not add a glossary, a naming note, or an acknowledgements paragraph about the borrowings.** This
document exists so the papers do not have to, and putting any of it into them would violate the
policy it explains. Checked 2026-08-28: Seldon, Narrativium and Muchness are all used without gloss
or apology anywhere in the eight papers, and the Adams epigraph in the short form is consistent with
that.
