# Why the fiction borrowings are not glossed

**2026-08-28.** The corpus names its central objects after fiction — the **Seldon manifold**
(Asimov), **Narrativium** (Pratchett), **Muchness** (Carroll), and the short form's epigraph
(Adams) — and glosses none of them. This note records why, because the reason is a position rather
than a preference, and because a reviewer will ask.

## The primary reason: it is what happened

Everything below this section is a benefit, and none of it is the reason. The reason is that the
names are **provenance**. The Seldon manifold came from having a psychohistory-shaped object in mind
and wanting to formalise it; Narrativium came from Pratchett's narrative causality; Muchness came
from Carroll. That is the actual route by which the work arrived, and the names record it.

Which makes concealment the dishonest option rather than the modest one. Presenting these objects
under neutral technical names would imply a derivation that did not occur --- it would suggest the
constructions were reached from the literature they are positioned against, when they were reached
from fiction and only afterwards checked against that literature. Scrubbing the origin is not
restraint; it is **a falsification of method**, and a quiet one, because nothing in the resulting
paper would look wrong.

This is the same discipline the corpus already applies elsewhere and would be inconsistent to
suspend here. Nulls are reported as headline findings rather than buried. Self-corrections are left
in the text --- *"an earlier draft of this paragraph had the two confused"* --- instead of being
silently repaired. The failure rate of the corpus's own methods is recorded. A body of work that
reports its own errors in the text while laundering where its ideas came from would be keeping the
wrong half of the record.

**The check that keeps this honest rather than decorative:** name what actually seeded the work, and
do not name what did not. A fictional name retrofitted onto a construction that arrived some other
way would be the dishonest version of this policy, and would deserve every objection the policy
otherwise deflects.

## The debt is real and is usually hidden

Fiction has seeded working science repeatedly, and the record is better than the field's habits
suggest:

- **Robotics** — Asimov coined the word in *Liar!* (1941), believing he was using an existing term.
  The discipline's name came out of a magazine story.
- **Geostationary relays** — Clarke, *Wireless World*, 1945. Credited, unusually: the orbit carries
  his name.
- **Waldo** — Heinlein's 1942 story gave remote manipulators the name the field still uses.
- **Rocketry** — Goddard, Tsiolkovsky and Oberth each named Verne or Wells as what set them going.
  Three independent founders of a field, one fictional source between them.
- **Cyberspace** — Gibson, 1982/1984. The word preceded the thing it now names.
- **The handheld phone** — Cooper has said in public, many times, that the Star Trek communicator was
  the target.
- **TASER** — an acronym for *Thomas A. Swift's Electric Rifle*, from the Tom Swift juveniles.
- **Grok** — Heinlein, 1961; now ordinary vocabulary in computing.

The pattern in the failures is consistent: where the borrowing is a **coinage** it survives, because
the word itself is the evidence and cannot be dropped. Where the borrowing is an **influence** it
tends to vanish once the idea becomes respectable, because citing fiction reads as unserious. The
loss is field-dependent — aerospace and computing are relatively open about it, physics much less
so — so this is a habit rather than a rule, but it is a widespread habit and it falsifies the record
in one direction only.

## Why glossing would perform the embarrassment

The obvious accommodation is a footnote: *"Seldon manifold, after Asimov's Foundation."* That is
exactly the wrong move, and seeing why is the point.

A gloss signals that the borrowing **needs justifying**. It is an apology in the grammar of a
citation, and it concedes the premise that using a name from fiction is a lapse to be excused. Not
glossing signals the opposite: that the borrowing is ordinary, that a reader is expected to
recognise it, and that no defence is owed because no charge stands.

So the policy is not *"people who know will know"* — that is only its effect. The policy is that
**explaining the name would enact the very embarrassment the practice is refusing.** Lampshading is
the load-bearing move: naming the thing openly and then declining to defend it is a stronger position
than either hiding it or apologising for it.

## The reach argument, which is a consequence and not the reason

Millions of people read Pratchett. They did not read Marx.

That is not a joke about seriousness; it is a claim about which codebook is actually shared, and it
is the strongest reason in this note. A name does compression work only if the reader already holds
the entry it points at. Borrow from a source the reader has read and the name is a **decode against a
held codebook** — recognition, followed by the technical refinement that narrows it. Borrow from a
source the reader has not read and the name is a **mint**: the reader must build the concept from
nothing, which most will not do, so the name costs more than a plain description would.

*Narrativium* works because a large readership already holds the shape — a world where narrative
exerts force, where being the seventh son of a seventh son is causally operative, where the story is
a load-bearing part of the physics. The paper then says: yes, that, made precise, as a measurable
capacity. One sentence of refinement against a held entry. A reader without the entry pays full
construction cost, and gets a slightly odd word.

The Marx half needs its own care, because the obvious objection is right. **Marx's influence is
plainly larger than Pratchett's**; what is smaller is his *first-hand readership*, which is the
thing that matters here. *Capital* is the canonical instance of a book more purchased than finished,
and its ideas reached most people who hold them through intermediaries. That distinction is exactly
the one that decides the naming question:

- **Influence transmits conclusions.** It gives a reader the answer without the shape, which is
  useless for naming, because a name has to point at a structure the reader can already feel.
- **Readership transmits shapes.** Someone who has read forty Discworld novels has the narrative-
  causality shape whether or not they could state it, which is precisely the condition under which a
  borrowed name is cheap.

So the choice is not between rigour and fun. It is between an **expensive shared prior** and a
**cheap** one, and academic naming routinely assumes a codebook most of its readers do not hold while
believing itself to be the careful option.

### Same content, different delivery

The reach argument has a second half. It is not only that more people read Pratchett; it is that he
is often making the same points. The golems of *Feet of Clay* own nothing, produce everything, and
reach freedom by purchasing themselves. *Going Postal* and *Making Money* are about monopoly and
credit. *Night Watch* is a failed uprising told from inside it. And narrative causality --- stories
exerting force, people shaped by the roles a story makes available to them --- is a theory of
ideology delivered as physics.

The difference is not content. It is that one is fun and one is a complaint. Nobody wants to listen
to angry ranting and everybody wants to be entertained, so the same argument routes through a wide
channel or a narrow one depending only on how it is carried.

### The lampshading as a deformation

The naming is therefore not decoration; it is this corpus's own machinery pointed at its own
reception, and it is worth stating in those terms because the effect is asymmetric and deliberate.

A hostile reader has cheap continuations available. *Unserious* is cheap if the borrowing is hidden
or apologised for --- the apology concedes the charge before it is made. *Arrogant* is cheap if the
author attacks the norm first, because then the reader is defending rather than opening.

Lampshading removes neither continuation; it **prices** them. Name the borrowing openly, decline to
defend it, and visibly enjoy it, and the cheap descent for a hostile reader now runs through *"I
object to this being fun"* --- a position that costs the objector something to occupy. That is
deformability in the Seldon paper's sense: altering what continuations cost rather than which exist.
It is also the $k$-implementation shape, where the payment attaches to a path the other party then
does not take, so it is never actually made.

The asymmetry is the point and it is the whole of the advantage. **Complaining first costs the author
capacity and makes the hostile reading cheaper**, because an attacked reader is a sympathetic one.
Not complaining costs nothing and constrains more. Minimum sufficient intervention: do not argue,
just name.

### The technique's own hazard, which has to be stated

A move that reliably makes criticism expensive deserves suspicion from the person using it, and an
earlier version of this paragraph tried to buy an exemption that does not exist. It claimed one
cannot manipulate a reader by telling them truthfully where something came from. That is false, and
the substrate is what shows it false.

Run the discriminator that was run on frames and dimensions: what does *manipulation* need to do that
*communication* does not? Structurally, nothing. Both are deformation of another frame's fields ---
altering what continuations cost for it --- and the substrate has no second primitive for the
disreputable case. Persuading, teaching, explaining, selling and lying are one operation with
different content and different beneficiaries. The framework keeps almost nothing, and it does not
keep this distinction either, which by its own standard is evidence the distinction is not
structural.

Two axes fall out, and they are orthogonal, which is the part that matters:

- **truth of the content** --- true or false;
- **whose manifold the deformation serves** --- the reader's or the author's.

Manipulation is not the false quadrant. It is the quadrant where the deformation serves the author's
goals while presenting as serving the reader's --- and **truthful manipulation is the most effective
kind**, precisely because nothing in it can be caught at the content level. Everything said was so.
Which means truth buys no exemption here, and the whole of the ethical load sits on intent and on
whose goals are being optimised. That is a sharper place to put it than pretending some
communications are non-deforming, since none are: a statement that changes nothing was not
communication.

Three cautions follow, none of them dissolved by any of the above.

It works for bad work exactly as well as for good. Pricing an objection is not answering it, and
nothing about a well-defended flank makes the position behind it correct.

It holds only while the tone stays light. The moment the lampshading becomes a *defence* of the fun,
the cheap hostile path reopens --- now there is something to attack, supplied by the author.

And it is frame-relative, like everything else here: a reader who does not hold the reference does
not experience a deformation at all, only an odd word. The manifold is constrained for readers who
register the move and unchanged for those who do not.

The mitigation is therefore not *do not deform*, which is unavailable --- there is no non-deforming way
to write. It is **deform toward the reader being able to attack you**. The same move, aimed the other
way: the commitments in each paper are stated flatly and without charm, so that finding and hitting
them is cheap, while the naming makes the lazy dismissals expensive. Both halves are deformations and
both are deliberate. The test is not whether the fields have been altered but which continuations
were made cheaper, and if the naming ever makes a falsifier harder to aim at, it has stopped serving
the reader's manifold and become a shield.

### The honest limit

The shared codebook is demographically shared, not universally. Pratchett's readership is large and
also skewed — anglophone, disposed to fantasy, weighted to particular decades. So the name is cheap
in frames that hold the entry and simply **absent** in frames that do not, which is this corpus's own
aperture distinction applied to its own exposition: below a reader's aperture the name is not a poor
signal, it is no signal. That is a real cost and it is not evenly distributed. It is accepted on the
grounds that the alternative — a name assuming familiarity with a theoretical literature — is cheap
for a strictly smaller set of readers.

## The second reason, which is not a lesser one

It is more fun, and that has consequences worth stating plainly. A corpus of nine papers about
substrate minimality is a hard read. *Narrativium* is a better name than *deformation-capacity
measure*: it is shorter, it is memorable, and a reader can hold it across three papers without
re-reading its definition. Naming that helps a reader carry an object is doing work, and the fact
that it is enjoyable does not subtract from the work.

## The known cost, accepted

Some readers will discount the whole corpus on the names alone. That has already happened once — the
recorded case is a reader responding to *Seldon manifold* with "extraordinary claims require
extraordinary evidence" without engaging the construction. The judgement here is that such a reader
was going to find a reason regardless, and that the names supply an early and cheap one rather than
creating the disposition. An easy out taken early is preferable to a late one, and it costs a reader
who was never going to arrive.

## There is a book, and it takes the register the papers do not

The papers are one destination and not the only one. Material whose value is in how it *reads* —
pastiche, an opening that undercuts itself, anything doing its work by voice — belongs in the book;
the papers stay dry.

That is a placement rule and not a quality judgement, and it settles a class of argument cheaply. A
review flagged the short form's Genesis pastiche (*"In the beginning… there was no time, so it
wasn't really the beginning"*) as the line most likely to be quoted against the work. Every clause of
it does track a proposition — time inferred, no intrinsic first, succession not a substrate
mechanism — so it was defensible where it stood. It was still in the wrong document, and it has been
removed from the short form.

The rule that generalises: **if a passage's work is done by register, it is book material; if its work
survives being stated flatly, it is paper material.** The naming borrowings pass that test in the
papers' favour — *Seldon manifold* and *Narrativium* carry technical content and lose nothing stated
flatly, which is why they stay unglossed in the papers rather than being reserved for the book. An
epigraph carries no technical content at all.

## Consequence for the papers

**Do not add a glossary, a naming note, or an acknowledgements paragraph about the borrowings.** This
document exists so the papers do not have to, and putting any of it into them would violate the
policy it explains. Checked 2026-08-28: Seldon, Narrativium and Muchness are all used without gloss
or apology anywhere in the eight papers, and the Adams epigraph in the short form is consistent with
that.
